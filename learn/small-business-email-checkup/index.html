Small-business email checkup: 5 settings, your domain, and holiday hours

TutorialSecurityPractical ITBeginner· 3 min read

For most small businesses, email is where the money, the customers and the password resets all live. That makes it the account most worth checking. Here are the settings to look at, a few decisions about your domain, and a quick list for when you close for a holiday.

5 email settings to check

1. A second login step on every mailbox

Every mailbox, including shared ones like info@ or billing@, should have two-step login turned on. Our 2FA guide has the steps for Google and Microsoft.

2. Forwarding rules you didn’t set up

After breaking into a mailbox, attackers sometimes add a hidden rule that forwards or deletes certain emails, like invoices. In Gmail, look under Settings → Forwarding and POP/IMAP and Filters and Blocked Addresses. In Outlook, look under Settings → Mail → Rules and Forwarding. Remove anything you don’t recognize.

3. Who has admin access

In Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, check the list of admins. Each should be a named person who still needs it. Past staff and past helpers should be removed. Anyone who helps with your IT should have their own admin login, not yours. Our guide to working with IT help explains why.

4. SPF, DKIM and DMARC for your domain

These are three settings (DNS records) on your domain that tell other email services which servers are allowed to send as you. They make your real emails more likely to land in the inbox and make it harder for scammers to send fake email that looks like it’s from your address. Google now requires bulk senders to have them and recommends them for everyone (Gmail: email sender guidelines (opens in new tab)).

If those three acronyms mean nothing to you, that’s normal. Your email provider’s help pages walk through it, or it’s a good job to hand to someone who has done it before.

5. Apps connected to your mailbox

Over time, lots of apps get permission to read or send from your email: schedulers, CRMs, old plug-ins. In your Google Account (Security → Your connections to third-party apps & services) or Microsoft account (Privacy → Apps and services), remove the ones you no longer use.

Gmail address or your own domain?

A free Gmail address is fine to start. An address at your own domain (you@yourbusiness.com):

  • matches your website and looks more established,
  • stays yours if you switch email providers,
  • lets you add addresses like hello@ or billing@ to one inbox,
  • makes staff handoffs cleaner, since the address belongs to the business,
  • and, with SPF, DKIM and DMARC set up, is harder for scammers to fake.

Don’t let your domain expire

If your domain expires, your website and your email can stop working. A two-minute check:

  1. Turn on auto-renew at the company where you registered the domain.
  2. Update the card on file so the renewal doesn’t fail.
  3. Know which account owns the domain. It should be the business’s account, not a past employee’s or helper’s personal one.

Stop the “what time works?” email chain

Share a booking link instead. Clients pick an open time, and it lands on your calendar. Google Calendar’s appointment schedules and Microsoft Bookings both do this, and both are included in many business plans.

Closed for a holiday? Update 3 places

  1. Google Business Profile: add special hours (opens in new tab) for the days you’re closed, so Google Search and Maps show them.
  2. Email auto-reply: say when you’re closed and when you’ll reply.
  3. Voicemail greeting or website banner: the same message, short.

Then set a calendar reminder to undo all three when you’re back.
