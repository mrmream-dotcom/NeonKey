Passwords and passkeys: what actually keeps your accounts safe

TutorialSecurityBeginner· 4 min read

A lot of the password advice most of us grew up with is out of date. Swapping an “a” for an “@” and changing everything every 90 days doesn’t do much. What does: passwords that are long, random and used on one account only, a password manager to remember them, and passkeys where you can use them.

Reused passwords are the real problem

When a website gets breached, the stolen email addresses and passwords don’t stay on that site. Criminals feed them into tools that try the same pairs on other sites: email, banking, shopping, social media. This is called credential stuffing.

If you used that password anywhere else, those accounts are now open too. That’s why “unique” matters more than “complicated.” One leak should only ever open one door.

Want to see if your email has shown up in a known breach? Our data breach guide walks through the free check and what to do next.

What makes a password strong

CISA, the US cybersecurity agency, boils it down to three things (CISA: Use strong passwords (opens in new tab)):

  • Long. At least 16 characters.
  • Random. Not a word, a name, a date or a pattern. A passphrase of five to seven random, unrelated words works too.
  • Unique. Used for one account only.

Length beats symbol swaps. A password like Fluffy2019! checks the old boxes (capital letter, number, symbol) but it’s a pet name plus a year plus an exclamation point, which is one of the first patterns people guess. Four or more random words, or a long password made by a password manager, is far harder to guess.

You don’t have to remember them

Nobody can remember dozens of long, random, unique passwords. That’s the job of a password manager:

  • It creates a strong, different password for every account.
  • It stores them, locked behind one strong main password (often called the master password).
  • It fills them in for you, and it won’t fill them in on a fake look-alike site, which is a nice bonus against phishing.

You don’t need anything fancy. The password managers built into iPhone, Android and the major browsers cover the basics, and well-known standalone apps add features like sharing with family. Pick one you’ll actually use, then turn on two-step login for the password manager itself.

Retiring the password notebook

If passwords live in a notebook or a note on your phone today, you don’t have to move everything in one night. Start with the accounts that matter most:

  1. Your email, since it can reset almost every other password.
  2. Your bank and other money accounts.
  3. Your phone carrier account, since it controls your phone number and text codes.

Give each one a new, unique password from the manager as you go. Then do the rest a few at a time.

Skip the 90-day password changes

NIST, the US standards agency that publishes the federal digital identity guidelines, says services should not force people to change passwords on a schedule and should not require mixes of character types. They should require a change when there’s evidence a password was compromised (NIST SP 800-63B (opens in new tab)).

For you, that means: change a password when there’s a reason, like a breach notice, a login you don’t recognize, or having shared it with someone. Otherwise, a long, unique password can stay put. (If your workplace still requires scheduled changes, follow its rules.)

Even better than a password: passkeys

A passkey lets you sign in with your face, fingerprint or phone PIN instead of typing a password. There’s nothing to type, nothing to reuse, and nothing for a fake site to steal, because a passkey only works on the real site it was made for. CISA calls the standard behind passkeys the only widely available phishing-resistant sign-in (CISA: More than a Password (opens in new tab)).

Google, Apple and Microsoft accounts all support passkeys, and so do a growing number of shopping, banking and social sites.

Turn one on

  1. Open your account’s security settings. For Google, that’s your Google Account (opens in new tab) → Security & sign-in; look for Passkeys (Google: sign in with a passkey (opens in new tab)). Microsoft and other sites put it in a similar spot.
  2. Choose Create a passkey and follow the prompts. Your phone or computer will ask for your face, fingerprint or device PIN.
  3. Sign out and back in once to test it.

What if I lose my phone? Passkeys made on an iPhone sync through iCloud Keychain, and passkeys made on Android sync through Google Password Manager, so a new phone signed in to the same account gets them back. Most sites also keep your password or other sign-in options as a backup. Keep your account recovery info current either way.

A 15-minute plan

  1. Pick a password manager (the built-in one is fine) and turn on two-step login for it.
  2. Give your email account a new, long, unique password.
  3. Turn on two-step login for your email. Our 2FA guide has the steps.
  4. Add a passkey to your Google, Apple or Microsoft account.
  5. Next time you sign in anywhere else, let the manager replace that password with a unique one.
