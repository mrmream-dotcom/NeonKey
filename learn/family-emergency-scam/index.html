"Hi Mom, this is my new number": how to handle family emergency scams

TutorialSecurityBeginner· 3 min read

A text or call that seems to come from your child, grandchild or another relative says they’re in trouble and need money fast, and please don’t tell anyone. The FTC calls these family emergency scams (FTC: family emergency scams (opens in new tab)). They work because they hit you in the heart before your head can catch up. One phone call ends them.

The one move: hang up or stop replying, then call your family member on the number you already have for them. Not the new number. Not a number the caller gives you.

How they usually start

  • “Hi Mom, I dropped my phone, this is my new number.” It starts friendly. The money ask comes a few messages later.
  • “Grandma, it’s me, I’m in trouble.” An accident, an arrest, a hospital bill, a bus ticket home.
  • A second voice: a “lawyer”, “police officer” or “doctor” who explains the emergency and asks for payment.
  • A voice that sounds just like them. Scammers can use AI to copy a voice from a short video clip, so a familiar voice isn’t proof on its own (FBI IC3 public service announcement, Dec 2024 (opens in new tab)).

The tells

  1. A new number plus a money request. On its own a new number is normal. Paired with “can you send me money”, it’s the classic opening.
  2. “Don’t tell Mom” or “keep this between us.” Secrecy is there to stop the one call that would end it.
  3. Hard-to-reverse payment. Gift cards, a wire transfer, crypto, a payment app, or cash picked up by a “courier”.
  4. Urgency. It has to happen now, before you can think or check.
  5. No name, or details that are a little off. Many of these messages are sent to thousands of numbers and work on anyone who answers.

Set up a family code word this week

A code word is a word or short phrase only your family knows, asked for on any “emergency” call or text. It beats a cloned voice, because a scammer can copy how someone sounds but not what only your family knows.

  1. Pick something easy to remember but not online. Not a pet’s name you’ve posted, not a street name.
  2. Tell everyone in person or on a call, not in a group chat that could be read later.
  3. Agree on the rule: “If you ask me for money in an emergency, I’ll ask for the word. No word, I call you back on your usual number.”
  4. Practice once. It feels silly for ten seconds and saves a lot more than that.

If you already sent money

  1. Contact whoever handled the payment right away: your bank for a wire or transfer, the payment app, or the company that issued the gift card. Ask them to stop or reverse it. Keep the card and receipt.
  2. Report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov (opens in new tab) and ic3.gov (opens in new tab).
  3. Expect a second call. Scammers often call back asking for more, or pretending to help you get the money back. Hang up and call the number you find yourself.

Not sure about a message?

Paste it into the free Family Shield check. It runs in your browser and nothing you paste is saved. For more ways to help a parent or grandparent, see the family tips and How to spot a scam.

This guide is general advice, not a promise about any one message. When money is involved, check the direct way.
