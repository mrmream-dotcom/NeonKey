Is this message a scam? Practice with 6 real-looking examples

TutorialSecurityBeginner

Someone sent you a message and it feels off. Here's how to tell, using six practice messages that look like the real thing. Pick an answer for each, then see the tells. It takes about three minutes.

The one rule for any message that feels off: don't tap. Go direct. Open the company's app, type its website yourself, or call a number you find yourself, not one from the message. That works even when a scam looks perfect.

Checking for a parent? See Family Shield ↓

Practice: scam or normal?

Every message below is a sample made up for practice. The numbers and web addresses are fake. Don't call or type them.

  1. Sample 1 · TextFrom: +1 (555) 010-4471
    USPS: Your package is on hold due to an incomplete address. Pay the $1.25 redelivery fee within 24 hours: usps-redeliver-help.top/track

    Scam: a fake delivery text

    • A tiny fee is the hook. The real goal is your card number.
    • The link is not usps.com. It just has "usps" in a made-up address.
    • A 24-hour deadline to stop you from checking.

    What to do: Don't tap. If you're expecting a package, type usps.com yourself and enter the tracking number from the seller's email. Forward the text to 7726, then delete it.

    Source: US Postal Inspection Service: smishing (opens in new tab)

  2. Sample 2 · TextFrom: +1 (555) 010-2290
    Hi Grandma it's me, I dropped my phone in the lake so this is my new number. I'm in a bit of trouble and need $400 for a bus home. Please don't tell Mom, I'll explain later ❤️

    Scam: a "new number" family emergency

    • A new number plus an urgent money request.
    • "Don't tell Mom" is there to stop you from checking.
    • No name. It works on anyone who has a grandchild.

    What to do: Call your grandchild, or their parent, on the number you already have. A family code word settles it in seconds. Never send money to a "new number" first.

    Source: FTC: family emergency scams (opens in new tab)

  3. Sample 3 · TextFrom: tolls-notice@ezpay-roads.info
    FINAL NOTICE: You have an unpaid toll balance of $6.99. Pay now to avoid a $50 late fee and suspension of your license: ezpay-roads.info/pay

    Scam: an "unpaid toll" text

    • Threats about your license over a few dollars.
    • A web address that isn't your state's toll agency.
    • It went out to people who never drove on a toll road.

    What to do: Don't tap. If you think you might owe a toll, log in to your toll account the way you normally do, or call the number on the agency's own website.

    Source: FTC: unpaid toll texts (opens in new tab)

  4. Sample 4 · TextFrom: Fraud Dept
    First Sample Bank: Did you try a $892.00 purchase at an electronics store? Reply YES or NO. A specialist will call you to verify your identity with the code we just sent.

    Scam: a fake fraud alert that wants your code

    • The "specialist" call is the scam: they ask you to read back a login code.
    • A big, scary charge you never made, so you act fast.
    • Your real bank never needs you to read a code back to them.

    What to do: Don't reply and don't share any code. Call the number on the back of your card, or open your bank's app, and ask them if anything is wrong.

    Source: FTC: spam text messages (opens in new tab)

  5. Sample 5 · Pop-upFrom: Windows Security Alert
    ⚠ Your computer is infected with 3 viruses. Your files and banking passwords are at risk. Do NOT close this window. Call Microsoft Support now: (555) 010-0199

    Scam: a fake virus warning with a phone number

    • Real security warnings from Microsoft or Apple never ask you to call a number.
    • "Do NOT close this window" is there to keep you on the page.
    • Big claims with no way to check them.

    What to do: Don't call. Close the browser (on Windows, Ctrl + Shift + Esc opens Task Manager if it won't close). Never let a caller connect to your computer.

    Source: FTC: tech support scams (opens in new tab)

  6. Sample 6 · TextFrom: 55501
    Your Sample Shop verification code is 482913. It expires in 10 minutes. Sample Shop will never call or text to ask for this code.

    Fits a normal message, if you just asked for it

    • No link and nothing to pay.
    • It warns you never to share the code, which real services do.
    • The catch: it only fits if you just tried to sign in yourself.

    What to do: Use the code only on the site or app you opened yourself, and never read it to anyone. If you didn't just try to sign in, someone may have your password: change it from the official app.

    Source: FTC: spam text messages (opens in new tab)

5 of the 6 were scams, and even the normal one came with a condition. That's the real lesson: no single detail proves a message is fine. A message only fits a normal one if you were expecting it, nothing is urgent, and you can check it the direct way.

The tells, in one list

  1. Pressure. A deadline, a threat, a fee that grows. Urgency is there to stop you from checking.
  2. Something slightly off. A web address with the company's name inside a different address, a number you don't know, no name in the greeting.
  3. A request for a code, a password or a payment. Especially gift cards, crypto, wire transfers or "read me the code we just sent."
  4. "Don't tell anyone." Secrecy is there to stop the one call that would end the scam.

More detail on each, plus QR codes, fake shops and AI voice calls, is in the full guide: How to spot a scam.

Already tapped, paid or shared a code?

  1. Paid by card or bank? Call the number on the back of your card or on your bank's own website now. The sooner they know, the more they can do.
  2. Shared a password or code? Change that password from the official app or website, and sign out other devices. Turn on two-step login if it's off (how to turn on 2FA).
  3. Paid with gift cards? Contact the company that issued the card right away and keep the card and receipt.
  4. Report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov (opens in new tab). Forward scam texts to 7726 (it spells SPAM), then delete them.

Helping a parent or another adult you love

Scams often aim at the person who doesn't have a "tech person" sitting next to them. Three things you can set up this week, for free:

  • A "send it to me first" rule. Agree that any message asking for money, a code or a login gets sent to you before they tap anything. No judgment, ever.
  • A family code word. A word only your family knows, asked for on any "emergency" call or text. It beats a cloned voice.
  • The numbers they trust, saved. Their bank, their carrier and you, saved in their contacts, so "go direct" is one tap.
