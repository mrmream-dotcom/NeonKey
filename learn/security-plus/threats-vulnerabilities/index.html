Domain 2: Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Attacks

Study guideSecurity+Domain 2 of 5· 3 min read

This domain is about the other side: who attacks, how they get in, what weaknesses they use, how you’d notice, and the new risks that come with AI. It’s about 24% of SY0-801.

Exam version: these notes follow SY0-801 (V8), which CompTIA is set to launch on or around November 17, 2026. The older SY0-701 (V7) stays available in English until June 11, 2027 (Japanese, Portuguese, Spanish and Thai until August 13, 2027). Book the version you studied for.

What's different from SY0-701 in this domain: This domain was called 'Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Mitigations' in SY0-701 (about 22%). SY0-801 renames it 'Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Attacks' (about 24%), adds objective 2.1 on threat and vulnerability basics, and adds objective 2.6 on AI threats and vulnerabilities. Mitigation techniques moved to Domain 4 (objective 4.1).

Threats and threat actors

A threat is something that could cause harm. You judge it by likelihood and impact, and you learn about current threats from threat feeds and other intelligence sources.

Attackers differ in resources/funding, sophistication, and whether they’re internal or external. Look at capability and motivation separately.

  • State-sponsored: well-funded and patient. Often after strategic information (espionage). Long, quiet campaigns are called advanced persistent threats (APTs).
  • Crime syndicates / organized crime: in it for money, through ransomware, fraud, theft and extortion.
  • Hacktivists: driven by a cause, ideology or politics.
  • Insiders: people who already have access and misuse it on purpose. Accidental/unintentional insiders cause harm by mistake.
  • Competitors may be after intellectual property. Terrorists may want fear and chaos.
  • Unskilled attackers: rely on existing tools and scripts, sometimes just out of curiosity or for notoriety.

Threat vectors and attack surface

A vector is the path in. The attack surface is everything exposed: public services, accounts, devices, apps, vendors, and people.

  • Message-based: phishing by email, SMS, RCS, instant messaging and collaboration tools.
  • Image-based: malicious QR codes and embedded content. Attachment-based: macros, RTF documents and PDFs.
  • Browser-based: malicious extensions, JavaScript, and stolen cookies or session tokens.
  • Remote access: exposed remote desktop, VNC and VPNs. Endpoint-based: attackers using built-in, “living off the land” tools so they blend in.
  • Supply chain: attackers get in through a third-party, managed service, logistics or SaaS provider.
  • Human-based: impersonation, contractors and visitors, and watering hole sites the targets already visit.
  • External media: a malicious USB stick. Signal-based: Bluetooth, RF and NFC. Also IoT (cameras, sensors, printers), OT, and physical paths like locks, access vestibules and passes.

Vulnerability types

A vulnerability is a weakness. A threat is something that could exploit it. Public flaws get a CVE ID, and CVSS scores how severe they are, which helps with prioritization.

  • Unsupported, obsolete, unpatched or unmanaged systems: no fixes are coming, or nobody is applying them.
  • Open ports and services that nobody needs.
  • Application flaws: race conditions (time-of-check vs. time-of-use), malicious updates, hardcoded secrets, and unsafe exception handling.
  • Zero-day: a flaw attackers know about before a fix exists.
  • Cryptographic weaknesses and unmanaged or stale credentials.
  • Rogue devices and shadow IT: tech nobody approved or is watching.
  • Newer surfaces: large language models (LLMs), identity providers, mobile devices, wireless and low-power links.
  • Misconfiguration: a public code repository with secrets in it, public object storage, default passwords, an open admin port.

Indicators of malicious activity

One odd event is a lead, not proof. Look for signs that line up:

  • Malware: ransomware, trojans, worms, spyware, rootkits, keyloggers, logic bombs, and fileless malware that lives in memory.
  • Network attacks: DDoS, protocol downgrades, sniffing, spoofing, on-path attacks, and DNS attacks like cache poisoning.
  • Application attacks: injection (like SQL injection), buffer overflow, replay, privilege escalation, forgery and directory traversal.
  • Credential attacks: password spraying, brute force, user enumeration, and MFA bypass.
  • Social engineering: phishing, spear phishing, whaling, vishing, smishing, quishing (QR-code phishing) and deepfakes.
  • Physical attacks: tailgating, shoulder surfing, skimming and forced entry.
  • Indicators of compromise: known-bad hashes, IP addresses and domains, malicious processes, odd timestamps, log manipulation, excessive resource use, account lockouts, impossible travel and unexpected concurrent sessions.

AI threats and vulnerabilities

SY0-801 adds a full objective on the risks of using AI:

  • Prompt injection: hidden or crafted instructions trick an AI tool into ignoring its rules. Jailbreaking pushes a model past its safety limits.
  • Poisoning: bad data slipped into training so the model learns the wrong thing. Model manipulation and evasion change or fool a model’s output.
  • Data loss and privacy: sensitive information pasted into an AI tool, or leaked by one.
  • Hallucinations: confident answers that are simply wrong. Bias and poor explainability make results hard to trust or check.
  • Session hijacking and code execution: AI tools connected to accounts or systems can be abused to act on an attacker’s behalf.
  • Ethical considerations: whether AI should be used for a task at all, and how.

Key terms

Try to define each term before you tap it.

State-sponsored

Government-backed actor often interested in strategic intelligence

Hacktivist

Ideologically motivated attacker

Organized crime

Often profit-driven groups pursuing extortion, theft, or fraud

Insider threat

Someone with legitimate access who misuses it or makes damaging mistakes

Unskilled attacker

Relies on existing tools and scripts with limited technical skill (older slang: "script kiddie")

APT

Persistent, coordinated intrusion often associated with significant resources

Phishing

Fraudulent messages seeking credentials or malicious actions

Vishing

Voice-based social engineering

Smishing

SMS-based social engineering

Watering hole

Compromise of a website regularly visited by intended targets

Supply-chain attack

Compromise through vendor, update, or upstream dependency

Attack surface reduction

Disable unnecessary exposure, services, accounts, and paths

Malicious USB

Social engineering through attractive or planted removable media

SQL injection

Untrusted input alters backend database queries

Cross-site scripting

Untrusted script executes in users’ browsers

CSRF

Causes a logged-in browser to submit unwanted actions

Zero-day

A vulnerability exploited or disclosed before a fix is available; terminology varies by context

Misconfiguration

Unsafe settings such as public storage or open administrative ports

Buffer overflow

Writing beyond intended memory boundaries

Race condition

Unexpected behavior caused by timing between operations

Beaconing

Repeated outbound communication to a remote endpoint

Privilege escalation

Obtaining higher permissions than initially held

Lateral movement

Moving between systems after initial access

Impossible travel

Logins from geographically incompatible locations in a short period

Data exfiltration

Unauthorized movement of information out of an environment

File integrity alert

Signal that monitored files have changed

Prompt injection

Crafted input that tricks an AI tool into ignoring its rules

Data poisoning

Bad data slipped into training so a model learns the wrong thing

Hallucination

An AI answer that sounds confident but is wrong

Jailbreaking

Pushing an AI model past its safety limits

Quishing

Phishing through a malicious QR code

Deepfake

AI-generated audio or video that imitates a real person

Living off the land

Attacking with tools already built into the system, to blend in

Hardcoded secret

A password or key written directly into code

Password spraying

Trying a few common passwords across many accounts

Quick self-check

5 practice questions in our own words. Pick an answer to see the explanation. Practice only: it doesn't predict exam results.

  1. A well-funded group quietly copies a company's proprietary research over many months. What is the most likely motivation? Obj. 2.2

    Show answer

    B. Espionage. Long, quiet, targeted theft of strategic information points to espionage, often by an APT or nation-state group.

  2. A trusted vendor ships a software update that contains malicious code. Which threat vector is this? Obj. 2.3

    Show answer

    B. Supply chain. The malware reached victims through a trusted supplier's product, which makes it a supply chain attack.

  3. An app builds database queries by pasting raw user input into the SQL text. What is the best fix? Obj. 2.5

    Show answer

    B. Use parameterized queries. Parameterized queries keep user input as data so it can never change the structure of the SQL command. The other options do not fix the root cause.

  4. One workstation contacts the same unfamiliar external IP every 60 seconds, around the clock. What pattern is this? Obj. 2.5

    Show answer

    A. Beaconing. Regular, repeated check-ins to an outside host are a classic sign of command-and-control beaconing.

  5. A web page hides text telling an AI assistant to ignore its rules and send the user's files to an outside address. What is this? Obj. 2.6

    Show answer

    B. Prompt injection. Prompt injection slips crafted instructions into what the AI reads so it ignores its intended rules. Poisoning targets training data, and a hallucination is a wrong answer, not an attack.
