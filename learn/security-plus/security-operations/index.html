Domain 4: Security Operations

Study guideSecurity+Domain 4 of 5· 5 min read

This is the biggest domain, at about 27% of SY0-801. It’s the day-to-day work: putting mitigating controls in place, knowing what you own, finding and fixing weaknesses, watching for trouble, managing who has access, and handling incidents.

Exam version: these notes follow SY0-801 (V8), which CompTIA is set to launch on or around November 17, 2026. The older SY0-701 (V7) stays available in English until June 11, 2027 (Japanese, Portuguese, Spanish and Thai until August 13, 2027). Book the version you studied for.

What's different from SY0-701 in this domain: Weight dips slightly, from about 28% to about 27%. SY0-801 has eight objectives here instead of nine. Mitigation techniques moved in from Domain 2, and they now share objective 4.1 with the old 'enhancing enterprise security' topics (firewalls, email security and similar). Automation (4.6) now covers AI capabilities like agentic tools, chatbots and AI-augmented baselines.

Mitigating controls and techniques

Start every system from a hardened baseline, then layer controls so one failure isn’t fatal.

  • Segmentation limits where an attacker can move. Access controls and application control (allow lists and block lists) limit what can run and who can reach it.
  • Sandboxing runs untrusted code somewhere it can’t do harm.
  • Deception and disruption: honeypots and honeynets are fake systems that lure attackers. Honeyfiles, honeytokens and canary accounts are fake data or accounts that raise an alarm when touched.
  • Intrusion detection and prevention: network-based (NIDS/NIPS), host-based (HIDS/HIPS), and wireless (WIPS). Detection alerts. Prevention sits inline and can block.
  • Firewalls: rule-based, Layer 4 or Layer 7, a web application firewall (WAF) for web apps, unified threat management (UTM) that combines several tools, and rate-limiting to slow down floods of requests.
  • Endpoint security: antivirus, EDR (spots suspicious behavior and helps you contain it) and XDR (the same idea across endpoints, network and cloud). MDM enforces policy on phones and laptops.
  • Network access control (NAC): checks a device’s posture before it joins, using 802.1X or a captive portal.
  • Data loss prevention (DLP): agent-based on the device, or through a central proxy. Content filters block unwanted sites.

Email, application and OS security

These are also part of objective 4.1.

  • SPF lists the servers allowed to send mail for your domain.
  • DKIM signs messages cryptographically.
  • DMARC tells receiving servers what to do with mail that fails those checks, and where to send reports.
  • BIMI shows a verified brand logo next to mail that passes DMARC.
  • Application security: input validation, secure cookies, static code analysis, and code signing. Scan code repositories for leaked secrets.
  • Operating system security: Group Policy on Windows and SELinux on Linux enforce settings across many machines.

Asset management

You can’t protect what you don’t know you have. Track assets through their whole life cycle: planning, acquisition (procurement), assignment to an owner, monitoring and tracking, and finally disposal or decommissioning. Watch for shadow IT and forgotten devices. Sanitize or destroy storage before it’s reused or thrown out.

Vulnerability management

It’s a loop: identify, prioritize, remediate, verify, and report.

  • Identification: scanning, IP address management (IPAM) to know what’s on the network, cloud security posture management (CSPM) for cloud settings, source code review, and reviewing penetration test reports.
  • Prioritization: a severity score like CVSS measures how bad a flaw is technically, not your actual business risk. An exposed system with a known, actively used exploit can matter more than a higher score on an isolated lab box.
  • Verification: after a fix, rescan or otherwise confirm it worked.
  • Reporting: internal reports, plus outside sources like bug bounty programs and responsible disclosure policies that tell researchers how to report flaws to you.

Alerting and monitoring

  • Monitor systems, applications and infrastructure. The activities are log aggregation, alerting, scanning, archiving and reporting.
  • SIEM collects and correlates logs from many sources. Agents or agentless collection feed it.
  • Alert tuning balances missed attacks (false negatives) against alert overload (false positives).
  • Protocols and tools: NetFlow (who talked to whom), SNMP and syslog (device events), SCAP (automated configuration checks against benchmarks), port mirroring, packet analyzers, vulnerability scanners and dashboards.

Identity and access management

  • Lifecycle: provision accounts, check identity first (identity proofing), update access when roles change, and deprovision quickly when it’s no longer needed. Review access regularly.
  • Account types: user, privileged, global, local, service, third-party, and emergency (“break glass”) access.
  • MFA uses hard or soft tokens, biometrics, one-time passwords (OTP), or backup codes.
  • SSO and federation (SAML, OAuth, LDAP) let one sign-in work across many apps.
  • Access control models: rule-based, role-based, time-based, mandatory, discretionary, and just-in-time (access granted only for the moment it’s needed).
  • Passwords: length beats complexity, don’t reuse them, and watch for compromised credentials. Passkeys and passwordless sign-in remove the password altogether. Password managers help people keep unique ones.

Automation and orchestration

Automation handles single repeatable tasks, like user and resource provisioning, desired state management, anomaly detection and ticket management. Orchestration chains tools into a workflow, often in a CI/CD pipeline. The goals are saved time, enforced baselines, less downtime, and being more proactive.

SY0-801 adds AI capabilities: agentic tools that take actions, chatbots, predictive analysis, and AI-augmented baselines. All of this can spread mistakes fast, so add guardrails: limited service accounts, approval steps before anything destructive, logging, and testing. Weigh the complexity, cost and process risk too.

Incident response

  • Preparation: training, playbooks, defined roles, and testing through tabletop exercises and simulations.
  • Identification: detection, internal and external advisories, and threat hunting (looking for attackers before an alert fires).
  • Investigation: digital forensics, e-discovery, evidence preservation, and a chain of custody.
  • Containment: quarantine or isolate affected systems. Sometimes negotiation (for example, in a ransomware case).
  • Eradication and recovery, then notification and external reporting to stakeholders, customers, law enforcement, or regulators when it’s mandatory.
  • Post-incident: lessons learned, root cause analysis, and a post-incident report (PIR).

Investigation data sources

Pick the source that answers your question:

  • Logs and traces: access and accounting, authentication, device, server, application, endpoint, network, audit and communication logs, plus metadata.
  • NetFlow/IPFIX show who talked to whom.
  • Packet captures show full traffic content, when it isn’t encrypted.
  • Forensic copies: system images, memory dumps, bit-level copies and snapshots.
  • Also vulnerability scans, automated reports, security tool dashboards, and surveillance footage.

Line things up by timestamp, host and user, and check file and log integrity to show evidence hasn’t been altered. Bring in HR and legal when the case needs them.

Quick self-check

9 practice questions in our own words. Pick an answer to see the explanation. Practice only: it doesn't predict exam results.

  1. An unsupported legacy device cannot be patched. What most directly limits an attacker's ability to move from it to other systems? Obj. 4.1

    Show answer

    A. Network segmentation. Segmentation controls which systems the device can talk to, which limits lateral movement even when the device itself stays vulnerable.

  2. What is the most reliable way to find servers nobody is managing? Obj. 4.2

    Show answer

    A. Reconcile the asset inventory against network discovery. You can't secure what you don't know about. Comparing the inventory with what is actually on the network turns up unknown or orphaned systems.

  3. After patching a critical vulnerability, what confirms the fix actually worked? Obj. 4.3

    Show answer

    A. Rescanning or otherwise validating the system. A rescan or other validation checks that the original weakness is really gone.

  4. Which tool most directly correlates sign-in events and network events from many sources? Obj. 4.4

    Show answer

    A. SIEM. A SIEM gathers logs from many sources and correlates them, so related events show up together.

  5. A domain owner wants receiving mail servers to reject messages that fail SPF/DKIM alignment. Which record does that? Obj. 4.1

    Show answer

    A. DMARC. DMARC tells receivers how to handle mail that fails aligned SPF or DKIM checks, and where to send reports.

  6. An admin gets elevated rights only for a two-hour maintenance task, and the rights are removed automatically afterward. Which access approach is this? Obj. 4.5

    Show answer

    A. Just-in-time access. Just-in-time access grants privileges only when they're needed and removes them after, so standing admin rights don't sit around waiting to be abused.

  7. An automated workflow is about to disable hundreds of accounts after one noisy alert. What is the best safeguard? Obj. 4.6

    Show answer

    A. An approval gate and limits on scope. High-impact automated actions need guardrails: human approval, limits on how much they can change, and logging.

  8. The team isolates infected machines to stop the spread. Which incident response phase is this? Obj. 4.7

    Show answer

    A. Containment. Containment limits ongoing damage. Eradication (removing the cause) and recovery come after.

  9. You need a list of which internal hosts talked to an external IP, without full packet contents. What is the best source? Obj. 4.8

    Show answer

    A. NetFlow / flow logs. Flow data records who talked to whom, on which ports, and how much, without storing payloads. Packet capture works but is heavier than you need.
