## Threats and threat actors

A **threat** is something that could cause harm. You judge it by **likelihood** and **impact**, and you learn about current threats from **threat feeds** and other **intelligence sources**.

Attackers differ in **resources/funding**, **sophistication**, and whether they're **internal or external**. Look at capability and motivation separately.

- **State-sponsored:** well-funded and patient. Often after strategic information (espionage). Long, quiet campaigns are called **advanced persistent threats (APTs)**.
- **Crime syndicates / organized crime:** in it for money, through ransomware, fraud, theft and extortion.
- **Hacktivists:** driven by a cause, ideology or politics.
- **Insiders:** people who already have access and misuse it on purpose. **Accidental/unintentional** insiders cause harm by mistake.
- **Competitors** may be after intellectual property. **Terrorists** may want fear and chaos.
- **Unskilled attackers:** rely on existing tools and scripts, sometimes just out of curiosity or for notoriety.

## Threat vectors and attack surface

A **vector** is the path in. The **attack surface** is everything exposed: public services, accounts, devices, apps, vendors, and people.

- **Message-based:** phishing by email, SMS, RCS, instant messaging and collaboration tools.
- **Image-based:** malicious QR codes and embedded content. **Attachment-based:** macros, RTF documents and PDFs.
- **Browser-based:** malicious extensions, JavaScript, and stolen cookies or session tokens.
- **Remote access:** exposed remote desktop, VNC and VPNs. **Endpoint-based:** attackers using built-in, "living off the land" tools so they blend in.
- **Supply chain:** attackers get in through a third-party, managed service, logistics or SaaS provider.
- **Human-based:** impersonation, contractors and visitors, and **watering hole** sites the targets already visit.
- **External media:** a malicious USB stick. **Signal-based:** Bluetooth, RF and NFC. Also **IoT** (cameras, sensors, printers), **OT**, and **physical** paths like locks, access vestibules and passes.

## Vulnerability types

A **vulnerability** is a weakness. A **threat** is something that could exploit it. Public flaws get a **CVE** ID, and **CVSS** scores how severe they are, which helps with prioritization.

- **Unsupported, obsolete, unpatched or unmanaged systems:** no fixes are coming, or nobody is applying them.
- **Open ports and services** that nobody needs.
- **Application flaws:** **race conditions** (time-of-check vs. time-of-use), malicious updates, **hardcoded secrets**, and unsafe exception handling.
- **Zero-day:** a flaw attackers know about before a fix exists.
- **Cryptographic weaknesses** and **unmanaged or stale credentials**.
- **Rogue devices** and **shadow IT:** tech nobody approved or is watching.
- **Newer surfaces:** large language models (LLMs), identity providers, mobile devices, wireless and low-power links.
- **Misconfiguration:** a public code repository with secrets in it, public object storage, default passwords, an open admin port.

## Indicators of malicious activity

One odd event is a lead, not proof. Look for signs that line up:

- **Malware:** ransomware, trojans, worms, spyware, rootkits, keyloggers, logic bombs, and **fileless malware** that lives in memory.
- **Network attacks:** DDoS, protocol downgrades, sniffing, spoofing, on-path attacks, and DNS attacks like cache poisoning.
- **Application attacks:** injection (like SQL injection), buffer overflow, replay, privilege escalation, forgery and directory traversal.
- **Credential attacks:** password spraying, brute force, user enumeration, and **MFA bypass**.
- **Social engineering:** phishing, spear phishing, whaling, vishing, smishing, **quishing** (QR-code phishing) and **deepfakes**.
- **Physical attacks:** tailgating, shoulder surfing, skimming and forced entry.
- **Indicators of compromise:** known-bad hashes, IP addresses and domains, malicious processes, odd timestamps, log manipulation, excessive resource use, account lockouts, **impossible travel** and unexpected concurrent sessions.

## AI threats and vulnerabilities

SY0-801 adds a full objective on the risks of using AI:

- **Prompt injection:** hidden or crafted instructions trick an AI tool into ignoring its rules. **Jailbreaking** pushes a model past its safety limits.
- **Poisoning:** bad data slipped into training so the model learns the wrong thing. **Model manipulation** and **evasion** change or fool a model's output.
- **Data loss and privacy:** sensitive information pasted into an AI tool, or leaked by one.
- **Hallucinations:** confident answers that are simply wrong. **Bias** and poor **explainability** make results hard to trust or check.
- **Session hijacking** and **code execution:** AI tools connected to accounts or systems can be abused to act on an attacker's behalf.
- **Ethical considerations:** whether AI should be used for a task at all, and how.
