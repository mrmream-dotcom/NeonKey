## Security controls

Every control can be described two ways: **how it's implemented** (its category) and **what it's meant to do** (its type or function).

- **Categories:** *technical* (enforced by hardware or software, like firewall rules or MFA), *managerial* (policies, risk assessments, planning), *operational* (things people do day to day, like awareness training or reviewing logs), and *physical* (fences, locks, bollards).
- **Types:** *preventive* stops something before it happens, *detective* spots or records it, *corrective* fixes things afterward, *deterrent* discourages an attacker from trying, *directive* tells people what to do, and *compensating* stands in when the preferred control can't be used.

A locked door is physical and preventive. A camera is physical and mainly detective. If an old app can't support MFA, putting it behind a jump host with extra monitoring is a compensating control.

## Core security ideas

- **CIA triad:** *confidentiality* (only the right people see it), *integrity* (it isn't changed without permission), and *availability* (it works when people need it).
- **AAA:** *authentication* proves who you are, *authorization* decides what you're allowed to do, and *accounting* records what you did.
- **Non-repudiation:** proof that someone really did something, usually through a digital signature, so they can't credibly deny it.
- **Least privilege:** give people and systems only the access they actually need.
- **Zero Trust:** don't trust anything just because it's "inside" the network. Check identity, device and context on every request.

## Change management

Most outages and plenty of security holes come from changes made in a hurry. A good change process includes an owner, a reason, an impact analysis, approval, a maintenance window, testing, a **backout (rollback) plan**, and documentation.

- *Standard* changes are pre-approved, low-risk and repeatable.
- *Normal* changes go through review and approval.
- *Emergency* changes get fast-tracked approval, then a review afterward.

Version control helps too: it shows who changed what, and it lets you roll back.

## Cryptography basics

- **Symmetric encryption** (like AES) uses one shared key. It's fast, so it's used for bulk data such as full-disk encryption.
- **Asymmetric encryption** (like RSA or ECC) uses a public/private key pair. It's used for key exchange and digital signatures.
- **Hashing** (like SHA-256) makes a one-way fingerprint of data. It isn't encryption. Use it to check whether something has changed.
- **Digital signatures:** you sign with your private key and anyone can verify with your public key. That gives integrity, authenticity and non-repudiation.
- **PKI:** certificates link an identity to a public key. Certificate authorities issue them and revocation lists (CRL/OCSP) cancel them.
- **Salting** adds random data to each password before hashing, which defeats precomputed "rainbow table" attacks.
