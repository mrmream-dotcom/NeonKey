## Security concepts and controls

Every control can be described two ways: **how it's implemented** (its category) and **what it's meant to do** (its type or function).

- **Categories:** *technical/logical* (enforced by hardware or software, like firewall rules or MFA), *managerial/administrative* (policies, risk assessments, planning), *operational* (things people do day to day, like awareness training or reviewing logs), and *physical/environmental* (fences, locks, bollards, climate control).
- **Types:** *preventive* stops something before it happens, *detective* spots or records it, *corrective* fixes things afterward, *deterring* discourages an attacker from trying, *directive* tells people what to do, and *compensating* stands in when the preferred control can't be used.

A locked door is physical and preventive. A camera is physical and mainly detective. If an old app can't support MFA, putting it behind a jump host with extra monitoring is a compensating control.

## Defense in depth

SY0-801 groups the core ideas under **defense in depth**: layers of protection, so one failure isn't fatal.

- **CIA triad:** *confidentiality* (only the right people see it), *integrity* (it isn't changed without permission), and *availability* (it works when people need it).
- **AAA:** *authentication* proves who you are, *authorization* decides what you're allowed to do, and *accounting* records what you did.
- **Non-repudiation:** proof that someone really did something, usually through a digital signature, so they can't credibly deny it.
- **Least privilege:** give people and systems only the access they actually need.
- **Zero Trust:** don't trust anything just because it's "inside" the network. Check identity, device and context on every request.

## Change management

Most outages and plenty of security holes come from changes made in a hurry. A good change process includes an owner, the stakeholders, an impact analysis, test results, approval from a **change advisory board (CAB)**, a maintenance window, a **backout plan**, and **standard operating procedures (SOPs)**.

- **Backout planning** means knowing how to undo the change. **Fail forward** means pushing a fix ahead instead of rolling back, when undoing would cause more harm.
- **Technical implications:** allow lists and deny lists may need updating, some activities may be restricted, and services or applications may need a restart or cause downtime. Watch legacy applications and dependencies.
- **Documentation:** update network diagrams and policies/procedures after the change.

Version control helps too: it shows who changed what, and it lets you roll back.

## Cryptography basics

- **Symmetric encryption** (like AES) uses one shared key. It's fast, so it's used for bulk data such as full-disk encryption.
- **Asymmetric encryption** (like RSA or ECC) uses a public/private key pair. It's used for key exchange and digital signatures.
- **Hashing** (like SHA-256) makes a one-way fingerprint of data. It isn't encryption. Use it to check whether something has changed.
- **Digital signatures:** you sign with your private key and anyone can verify with your public key. That gives integrity, authenticity and non-repudiation.
- **Encryption levels:** you can encrypt a full disk, a partition, a volume, a single file, a database, or a single record. Pick the level that matches what you're protecting.
- **PKI:** certificates link an identity to a public key. Certificate authorities issue them (after a certificate signing request, or CSR), and revocation lists (CRL) and OCSP report the ones that were canceled. A *wildcard* certificate covers the subdomains at one level, like *.example.com. A *self-signed* one isn't vouched for by any outside authority. **Key escrow** keeps a copy of a key with a trusted party.
- **Salting** adds random data to each password before hashing, which defeats precomputed "rainbow table" attacks.
- **Obfuscation** makes data or code harder to read or understand, without being true encryption.
