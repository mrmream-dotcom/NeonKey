## Governance

Governance is built from documents, called **artifacts**:

- **Policy:** the high-level rule (for example, "sensitive data must be encrypted"). Common ones cover acceptable use (AUP), BYOD, information security, incident response, access control, data classification and retention, data disposal, vulnerability disclosure, privacy, and a clean desk.
- **Standard:** the required specifics, like password, encryption or physical security standards.
- **Procedure:** the step-by-step instructions, like a standard operating procedure (SOP) or a runbook.
- **Guideline:** recommended, but not required. Benchmarks, advisories, implementation guides and reference architectures fit here.
- **Plans:** business continuity and disaster recovery.

Governance also defines who owns what, how often documents are reviewed, and how exceptions are handled.

## Risk management

Start by identifying assets and who owns each risk. Then assess it: risk is **likelihood × impact**, scored **qualitatively** (high, medium, low) or **quantitatively** (in dollars). Record it in a **risk register** with the owner and current mitigations, and review it regularly.

You can **avoid**, **mitigate**, **transfer** (for example, with insurance), or **accept** a risk. **Risk appetite** is how much risk leadership is willing to take on. **Residual risk** is what's left after your controls.

Quick math:

- **SLE** (single loss expectancy) = asset value × exposure factor
- **ALE** (annualized loss expectancy) = SLE × **ARO** (annualized rate of occurrence)

So a $20,000 loss expected every other year (ARO 0.5) gives an ALE of $10,000. These are estimates, not forecasts. A **business impact analysis (BIA)** identifies what's critical and how long it can be down.

## Third-party risk

A vendor's security becomes part of yours.

- **Vendor selection:** requests for proposal (RFP), information (RFI) or quote (RFQ), and expressions of interest (EOI). Do **due diligence** and check for conflicts of interest before signing.
- **Agreements:** **SLAs** (service targets) and SLOs, MOUs and MOAs, **NDAs** (confidentiality), a master services agreement (MSA), and a statement of work (SOW).
- **Monitoring:** keep a vendor registry, keep a **right to audit**, track service levels, and ask for compliance attestations or penetration test results.
- **Limits:** jurisdiction, laws, staffing, cost (ROI), and **vendor lock-in**.

## Compliance and privacy

Being compliant isn't the same as being secure. Know which rules actually apply to you, and train people on them (like data handling, anti-money laundering, and anti-bribery rules).

- **Monitoring:** attestations and signed acknowledgements show people know the rules.
- **Consequences of non-compliance:** fines, sanctions, legal and contractual trouble, reputational damage, or even loss of license.
- **Privacy:** people may have a **right to be forgotten**, a choice to opt in or opt out, and a right to have their data corrected or its processing restricted. The **controller** decides how personal data is used. The **processor** handles it on the controller's behalf.
- **Legal:** a **legal hold** means you must keep certain data, even if your normal retention schedule says to delete it.

## Audits and assessments

- An **audit** checks evidence against defined criteria, using sampling, questionnaires and interviews. It can be internal (including self-assessments and an audit committee) or done by an independent outside party, sometimes for a regulator.
- A **gap analysis** compares where you are with where a standard says you should be.
- A **penetration test** actually tries to exploit weaknesses, with permission. It can be *known environment*, *unknown environment* or *partially known*, and offensive, defensive or integrated. Reconnaissance can be passive or active.
- **Reference sources** like **MITRE ATT&CK**, the **Cyber Kill Chain** and the **Diamond Model** describe how attackers operate.
- Every finding needs an owner and a fix-by date.

## Security awareness

Training works best when it fits people's roles and focuses on real behavior: spotting social engineering and business email compromise (BEC), handling passwords and removable media, working safely from home, and reporting quickly.

- **Types:** onboarding, ongoing, targeted, and corrective training.
- **Delivery:** a learning management system (LMS), self-service portals, one-to-one or one-to-many sessions.
- **Measuring it:** use metrics, managerial reports and personnel behavior risk scoring. Measure **reporting rates and speed**, not just course completion. Phishing simulations should teach, not shame.
