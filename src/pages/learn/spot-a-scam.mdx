<p class="lead">Scams change their costumes all the time, but most of them want the same few things: a fast click, a password or code, or a payment that's hard to get back. Learn those tells and you can spot most scams, whatever they're dressed up as.</p>

## The three tells

CISA, the US cybersecurity agency, says most phishing messages give themselves away with a few signs ([CISA: Recognize and report phishing](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/recognize-and-report-phishing)). We group them into three:

1. **Pressure.** "Your account will be closed today." "Act now." Urgency is there to stop you from thinking.
2. **Something slightly off.** A sender address, a link or a phone number that's close to the real one but not quite.
3. **A request for a password, a code or a payment.** Especially an unusual payment method.

When something feels off: **don't tap. Go direct.** Open the company's app, or type its website yourself, and check there. Then report the message and delete it.

## See where a link goes before you tap it

- **On a phone:** press and hold the link to preview the address.
- **On a computer:** hover over the link and check the address that appears in the bottom corner of the window.

If the address doesn't match the company, skip it and open the official app instead.

**The padlock isn't a safety badge.** The padlock next to a web address means your connection to the site is encrypted. It doesn't tell you who's on the other end, and scam sites can have one too. Check that the address is spelled exactly right.

## Gift card payments are always a scam

The FTC is blunt about this: anyone who demands payment by gift card is a scammer ([FTC: gift card scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/avoiding-and-reporting-gift-card-scams)). It doesn't matter if they say they're the IRS, the police, tech support, a utility or your boss "needing cards for a client today."

- **Don't buy the cards,** and never read the numbers off the back to anyone.
- **Already paid?** Contact the company that issued the gift card right away, keep the card and the receipt, and report it at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/).

## Fake virus warnings with a phone number

A pop-up that says your computer is infected and tells you to call a number is a scam. Real security warnings from Microsoft or Apple don't ask you to call them ([FTC: tech support scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-spot-avoid-and-report-tech-support-scams)).

<ol class="steps">
  <li>Don't call the number, and never give a caller remote access to your computer.</li>
  <li>Close the browser. If it won't close, open Task Manager on Windows (<strong>Ctrl + Shift + Esc</strong>) or Force Quit on a Mac (<strong>Command + Option + Esc</strong>) and close it from there.</li>
  <li>Already let someone in? Disconnect from the internet, run your security software, and change your passwords from a different device.</li>
</ol>

## QR codes: read before you open

Scammers stick fake QR codes on parking meters, flyers and signs to send people to look-alike payment pages ([FTC consumer alert on QR codes](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/12/scammers-hide-harmful-links-qr-codes-steal-your-information)).

- Look for a sticker placed over the real code.
- Your camera shows the link before opening it. Read it.
- Paying for parking or a bill? Use the official app, or type the site yourself.

## Never share a login code

A code that's texted or emailed to you is the key to your account. Your bank, your carrier and tech support won't call and ask you to read it back. Anyone who does is trying to sign in as you. If you get a login code or a sign-in request you didn't ask for, someone may have your password: change it.

## Spam from a stranger? Don't hit unsubscribe

In a sketchy email, the unsubscribe link can just confirm your address is real, or lead to a phishing page. Mark it as spam instead. Use unsubscribe only for senders you recognize and signed up for.

## Fake delivery texts: forward to 7726

"Your package couldn't be delivered, update your address here" is one of the most common scam texts, especially around the holidays.

- Don't tap the link. Track real packages in the carrier's or store's app, or type its website yourself.
- Forward the text to **7726** (it spells SPAM on the keypad). That reports it to your mobile carrier. Then delete it ([FTC: spam text messages](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-and-report-spam-text-messages)).

## "Your order is confirmed." You didn't order it.

Fake order and renewal emails show a big charge you didn't make, then offer a "cancel order" button or a phone number to call. Both lead to the scammer.

- Don't click "cancel" and don't call the number in the email.
- Open the store's app, or type its site yourself, and check your real orders there.

## Fake shops and "90% off" deals

Before you pay a store you don't know:

<ol class="steps">
  <li><strong>Read the web address</strong> letter by letter. Look-alike spellings are common.</li>
  <li><strong>Search the shop's name</strong> plus the word "reviews" or "scam."</li>
  <li><strong>Look for real contact info:</strong> a physical address, a phone number, a return policy.</li>
  <li><strong>Pay by credit card,</strong> never by gift card, wire transfer or payment app to a stranger. Credit cards generally give you the strongest way to dispute a charge.</li>
  <li><strong>Go direct</strong> instead of tapping the ad. Type the store's address yourself.</li>
</ol>

**Turn on purchase alerts.** Many bank and card apps can notify you about every charge, so one you don't recognize shows up in seconds. Look under Alerts or Notifications in your bank's app.

## AI voice calls and a family code word

The FBI warns that criminals use AI to make audio that sounds like a loved one in a crisis, asking for money right away. Its advice includes creating a secret word or phrase with your family to verify who's calling ([FBI public service announcement, Dec 2024](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203)).

- **Pick a family code word** tonight, in person or on a call. Don't post it or text it to the group.
- If an urgent call asks for money and the caller can't say the word, **hang up and call back** on the number you already have for that person.

## Where to report

- Scams and fraud: [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/)
- Spam texts: forward to 7726
- Phishing emails: use your email app's "Report phishing" or "Report spam" button
- Identity theft: [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/)
