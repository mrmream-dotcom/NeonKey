<p class="lead">A text asks if you just made a big purchase. You reply NO, and a friendly "fraud specialist" calls to help. To "verify you", they ask you to read back a code your bank just texted. That code is the key to your account, and the specialist is the scammer. The FTC warns about texts like this ([FTC: spam text messages](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-and-report-spam-text-messages)).</p>

<div class="callout">
**The rule:** never read a code to anyone who contacted you. Hang up and call the number on the back of your card.
</div>

## How the trick works

1. **They already have part of your login,** often from a data breach or an earlier scam.
2. **They try to sign in as you,** which makes your bank send you a real one-time code.
3. **They call or text pretending to be the bank,** and ask you to read that code back "to verify you" or "to stop the charge".
4. **With the code, they're in.** They may change your password, add a payee or move money.

The code is real, which is what makes this one convincing. The person asking for it isn't.

## The tells

1. **"Did you try a $___ purchase? Reply YES or NO."** Replying starts the script and confirms your number.
2. **A request to share a code,** a PIN or your online banking password. Real codes usually say "never share this code" right in the message.
3. **Caller ID that looks right.** Scammers can fake the number that shows up, so it isn't proof.
4. **Pressure to act now** or move money to a "safe account". There's no such thing as a safe account a bank asks you to move money into.

## What to do instead

1. **Don't reply, don't tap, don't call back** the number in the message.
2. **Call the number on the back of your card,** or open your bank's app, and ask whether there's really a problem.
3. **Report the text:** forward it to **7726**, then delete it.

## If you already shared a code or a password

1. **Call your bank now** on the number on your card. Tell them exactly what you shared and when. Ask them to lock the account, stop pending transfers and reset your login.
2. **Change your online banking password** from the bank's app or a website you typed yourself, and the password on your email too if it's the same.
3. **Check for changes:** new payees, new devices, a changed phone number or email on the account.
4. **Report it** at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/), and follow [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/) if they got personal details.
5. **Turn on stronger sign-in** where your bank offers it. An authenticator app or passkey is harder to talk out of you than a text code (<a href={siteUrl('/learn/two-factor-authentication/')}>how to turn on 2FA</a>).
