Chrome, Firefox and iPhone All Got Security Fixes This Week. Here's How to Update.

Oct312026
By NeonKeySaturday, October 31, 20265 min readNewsSecuritySource: Google Chrome Releases (Oct 27, 2026) (opens in new tab)

If your phone or browser has been nudging you to restart lately, there's a good reason. In the last week of October, Google, Mozilla and Apple all released security updates for some of the most widely used software on the planet. None of it is cause for panic, but it's a good week to spend five minutes updating.

First, a few quick definitions

A security update (often called a patch) is a small fix that closes a weakness in a piece of software. Those weaknesses are called vulnerabilities. Think of them as unlocked windows that a criminal could climb through if they found them first.

Companies rate how serious each one is. A critical rating usually means a flaw could let someone run their own code on your device just because you visited a booby-trapped website. A zero-day is a flaw that attackers found and used before the company had a fix ready.

What came out

Google Chrome. On October 27, Google released Chrome 156 (opens in new tab) for Windows, Mac and Linux. It includes 52 security fixes, and four of them are rated critical. Google says the update "will roll out over the coming days/weeks," which means not everyone gets it on the same day.

Mozilla Firefox. The same day, Mozilla released Firefox 159 (opens in new tab) with fixes for 167 vulnerabilities. That number sounds alarming, but part of the jump comes from a bookkeeping change. Mozilla says it now lists each internally found bug separately instead of bundling them together. People on Firefox's long-term business version, called ESR, should look for 153.6 or 115.44.

Apple iPhone, iPad and Mac. On October 26, Apple released iOS 27.2, macOS 27.2 and a batch of other updates (opens in new tab). SecurityWeek counted (opens in new tab) 286 vulnerabilities fixed across the newest versions of iOS and macOS. Apple also put out iOS 26.7.2 for people who haven't moved to iOS 27 yet.

The one to pay special attention to: older iPhones

Apple also updated some older devices, specifically the iPhone XS, iPhone XS Max, iPhone XR and the 7th-generation iPad. Their new version, iOS 18.7.11, brings over a fix Apple first shipped for newer software in September.

That fix is for a zero-day. Apple says it is "aware of a report that this issue may have been exploited in an extremely sophisticated attack against specific targeted individuals on versions of iOS before iOS 27," according to BleepingComputer (opens in new tab). In everyday terms, the attacks Apple knows about were aimed at a small number of specific people, not the general public. Still, once a fix is public, it makes sense for everyone with one of those devices to install it.

Neither Google nor Mozilla said the browser flaws fixed this week were being used in attacks.

A side note: AI helped find some of these bugs

Here's a small, hopeful detail. Google's release notes credit some of the Chrome bugs to researchers working with AI tools from Anthropic and OpenAI, and Malwarebytes notes (opens in new tab) that both Google and Mozilla credit OpenAI's Codex Security tool with finding bugs fixed in these releases. People still verified and fixed each one, but AI is helping the good guys find weak spots faster.

What this means for you

You don't need to understand any of the technical details. You just need the updates installed. Here's how:

  • Chrome on a computer: Click the three-dot menu, then Help > About Google Chrome. Chrome checks for an update. If you see a Relaunch button, click it. Google's help page (opens in new tab) says your open tabs come back afterward, but private (Incognito) windows don't, so save anything you're working on first.
  • Firefox on a computer: Open the menu, then Help > About Firefox, and click Relaunch once it finishes downloading. You should see version 159 or later.
  • iPhone or iPad: Go to Settings > General > Software Update and tap Download and Install if an update appears. Apple's instructions (opens in new tab) suggest backing up first and plugging in to power on Wi-Fi.
  • Chrome on your phone: Check for an app update in the App Store or Google Play.

A few habits make this easier going forward:

  • Turn on automatic updates. On an iPhone, that's under Software Update > Automatic Updates. Browsers update themselves too, but only finish the job when you close and reopen them.
  • Restart now and then. If you leave your browser open for weeks, a waiting update never gets applied.
  • Only update from the real source. Use the browser's own About page or your phone's settings. Skip any pop-up or ad offering a "browser patch" download, since fake update prompts are a common trick.
  • If you run a small business, spend a few minutes this week making sure everyone's laptops and phones are current, including that one older iPhone in a drawer that still gets used.

The bottom line

Updates like these happen all the time, and that's a good thing. It means companies are finding and fixing problems. The simplest, cheapest security step most people can take is to let those fixes land, and this week is a perfect excuse to check.
