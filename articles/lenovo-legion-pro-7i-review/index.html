Lenovo Legion Pro 7i Gen 10 Review: A Gaming Laptop I Use for Work

ReviewsBy Michael ReamOct 30, 2026
Disclosure: I bought this laptop with my own money. Lenovo didn't sponsor this review, provide the laptop, or see this post before it went up.

For my first review, I wanted to cover something I actually use to do my work. That's the Lenovo Legion Pro 7i Gen 10, and I've had it for about six months.

Yes, it's a gaming laptop. But it has other strengths too. I use it for virtualization, for keeping a lot of applications open at the same time, and for driving two external monitors at my desk. It's handled all of that well. It also has real drawbacks, and I'll cover those too.

Specs are from Lenovo's PSREF spec sheet (opens in new tab) for the launch RTX 5080 model. Lenovo also lists a 2026 refresh under the same model name with a Core Ultra 9 290HX Plus (opens in new tab). If you're shopping, check the exact model number (it looks like 83F5xxxxUS) before you buy.

Price: As of Oct 30, 2026, Lenovo.com lists the Core Ultra 9 275HX / RTX 5080 / 32GB / 1TB configuration at $3,674.99. The 2026 refresh with the Core Ultra 9 290HX Plus and an RTX 5080 is listed at $3,399.99, and the cheapest configuration (with an RTX 5070 Ti) starts at $3,149.99. Lenovo's prices change often, so check the Legion Pro 7i page on Lenovo.com (opens in new tab) for today's number. For reference, launch reviews of RTX 5080 configurations listed $3,204 (discounted from $3,559, per Tom's Hardware (opens in new tab)) and $3,599 as tested (PCWorld (opens in new tab)). In March 2026, PC Gamer (opens in new tab) spotted a 32GB/2TB RTX 5080 model at $2,549 at B&H. The Amazon listings I've seen for this laptop were third-party "upgraded" bundles priced well above Lenovo's own store, so I'd buy direct or from an authorized retailer.

What it's good at for work

Virtualization

This is the main reason I'd recommend it beyond gaming. In my experience it handles virtualization well. On paper, the reason is simple: my CPU, the Core Ultra 9 275HX, has 24 cores, and Intel's spec page (opens in new tab) confirms support for VT-x, VT-x with EPT and VT-d. Those are the hardware features Hyper-V, WSL2, VMware and VirtualBox rely on. It's not a vPro chip, so there's no Intel AMT remote management.

If virtualization doesn't work out of the box, check that it's turned on in the BIOS. For bigger VM workloads, the two RAM slots let you upgrade later (PSREF lists 64GB max).

Multitasking

I keep a lot open at once, and this machine doesn't complain. I don't think about closing things to free up resources. The RTX 5080 can also speed up GPU-accelerated work like video exports and local AI tools, not just games.

Multi-monitor desk

I run two external monitors with it, and it handles them with ease. The port layout helps. According to Lenovo's PSREF, there are three separate video outputs: HDMI 2.1, a Thunderbolt 4 port with DisplayPort 2.1, and a USB-C port with DisplayPort 2.1. That suggests room for a third external screen, which I haven't tested.

The built-in OLED is factory calibrated, which helps for photo or video work.

The honest downsides

Weight. This is the big one for me. The weight isn't ideal, and it's definitely not a go-to if you're looking for portability. Reviewers measured it at about 5.8–6 lb (Notebookcheck (opens in new tab), PCWorld (opens in new tab), Tom's Hardware (opens in new tab)). Notebookcheck weighed the 400 W power brick at 2.66 lb on its own, so you're carrying roughly 8.5 lb in total.

Battery life. This isn't a laptop you take somewhere and work unplugged all day. Reviewers report about 4.5 to 5 hours of web browsing (Tom's Guide (opens in new tab): 4:37; LaptopMedia (opens in new tab): 5:05), and around 3 hours of normal use according to PCWorld. Gaming on battery lasts about an hour (PC Gamer (opens in new tab)).

Preinstalled software and ads. Lenovo's own datasheet (opens in new tab) lists a McAfee LiveSafe 30-day trial, Lenovo Antivirus, Power2Go and a Microsoft Office trial among the preloaded software. Tom's Hardware put it bluntly: "Ads are pervasive." It called out the Lenovo apps that push subscriptions and deals. You can uninstall what you don't want, but it shouldn't be there at this price.

No fingerprint reader. PSREF lists no fingerprint reader, and Tom's Hardware and PCWorld both note there's no Windows Hello biometric login. You're typing a PIN or password.

Fan noise. Notebookcheck flagged high core temperatures and fan noise under load.

Price. It's expensive. If you don't need the GPU, you're paying for hardware you won't use.

Don't need this much laptop?

Most everyday work doesn't need an RTX 5080. If you want something for email, documents and video calls without paying gaming-laptop money, I put together a few smaller-tier options.

Who should buy it, and who shouldn't

Consider it if:

  • You work mostly at a desk and want a laptop that can stand in for a powerful desktop
  • You run virtual machines or keep many heavy apps open at once
  • You use two or more external monitors
  • You also game or do GPU work and want one machine for all of it

Skip it if:

  • You carry your laptop every day. The weight and the brick will wear on you
  • You need all-day battery life
  • You want fingerprint or face login
  • Your work is mostly email, documents and browsing. One of the smaller-tier picks will cost a lot less
Verdict

The Legion Pro 7i Gen 10 is marketed as a gaming laptop. After six months of using it for work, I think that undersells it. It handles virtualization and heavy multitasking well, and it runs my two external monitors with ease. The weight is the real tradeoff. It's definitely not a go-to if you're looking for portability, and battery life, per reviewers, is short. Expect to spend a few minutes removing preinstalled trialware when you set it up.

If you need a powerful machine for your desk and can live with the weight, it's a strong pick. You can check current pricing on Lenovo's Legion Pro 7i page (opens in new tab).

— Michael Ream
